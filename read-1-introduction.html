1 Introduction

A subgraph of an edge-coloured graph is rainbow if no two of its edges have the same colour. The name is recent, but the questions are old. In 1723 Ozanam [230] published a card puzzle that asks for the sixteen court cards and aces to be laid out in a square so that no row or column repeats a rank or a suit. In 1782 Euler [120] asked for the same arrangement of thirty-six officers from six regiments and six ranks. Both puzzles ask for many disjoint rainbow perfect matchings at once. Most of this survey concerns three questions of this kind: when a rainbow copy of a given graph exists, how many disjoint copies there are, and when the host decomposes into them. Each time a natural statement fails, the obstructions are described before the positive results.

Latin squares are the first source of such questions. A Latin square of order $n$ is an $n\times n$ array filled with $n$ symbols, each appearing once in every row and column, and a transversal is a set of $n$ cells with no repeated row, column or symbol. If rows and columns are the two sides of $K_{n,n}$ and each edge is coloured by the symbol in its cell, transversals become rainbow perfect matchings (Figure 1). Euler's officers ask for a Latin square of order $6$ that decomposes into transversals. Tarry [261] showed in 1900 that none exists, and in 1959–60 Bose, Shrikhande and Parker [57,58] showed that every order other than $2$ and $6$ admits one (Section 2.2). For even $n$ the cyclic square has no transversal at all, which led Ryser in 1967 to conjecture a transversal for every odd order, and Brualdi and Stein to conjecture a partial transversal with $n-1$ cells for every order (Conjecture 2.19). The lower bounds then rose step by step: $n-\sqrt n$ in 1978 [68,270], $n-O(\log^2n)$ by Shor [248], $n-O(\log n/\log\log n)$ by Keevash, Pokrovskiy, Sudakov and Yepremyan [174], and finally $n-1$ for large $n$ by Montgomery [214] (Theorem 2.21). Ryser's statement for odd $n$ is still open. Random squares behave differently again: van Rees conjectured in 1990 that a random Latin square typically has no decomposition into transversals, but Bowtell and Montgomery [62] proved that it almost surely has one (Theorem 2.2).

The colouring of a Latin square is proper: edges at a common vertex have distinct colours. Other assumptions control colour repetition in other ways. A colouring is locally $k$-bounded if each colour appears at most $k$ times at each vertex, and globally $k$-bounded if each colour appears at most $k$ times in total; proper colourings are the locally $1$-bounded ones. Global bounds have a long history of their own. Erdős and Spencer [118] introduced the lopsided local lemma in 1991 to show that an $n\times n$ array in which no symbol appears more than $(n-1)/16$ times has a full transversal. Hahn and Thomassen conjectured in 1986 that colour classes of size $\alpha n$ force a rainbow Hamilton cycle in $K_n$, which Albert, Frieze and Reed proved. Frieze and Krivelevich asked for the same with bounded-degree spanning trees, and Böttcher, Kohayakawa and Procacci [59] answered this for every spanning graph of maximum degree $\Delta$, with colour classes of size $n/(51\Delta^2)$ (Theorem 5.5). Counting explains why such bounds are needed: a proper colouring of $K_n$ may use only $n-1$ colours, too few for a rainbow spanning graph with more edges. A fourth condition asks only for many colours at each vertex. Li [193] showed that minimum colour degree $(n+1)/2$ forces a rainbow triangle, and the colour-degree threshold for a spanning tiling by rainbow triangles is now known to lie between $5n/7$ and $5n/6$ [201].

Trees bring in graph labellings. At the 1963 symposium in Smolenice, Ringel conjectured that $K_{2n+1}$ decomposes into $2n+1$ copies of any tree with $n$ edges, and in 1967 Rosa [245] introduced graceful labellings as a way to prove it. Colour the edges of a complete graph on the integers by the differences of their ends. A graceful labelling is then a rainbow copy of the tree, and a rainbow copy in the cyclic version of this colouring rotates to a decomposition (Figure 11). Montgomery, Pokrovskiy and Sudakov [219] found such rainbow copies and proved Ringel's conjecture for large $n$; Keevash and Staden [175] proved it independently by another method (Theorem 3.14). The graceful tree conjecture is still open. Decompositions into rainbow spanning trees have a similar history. Brualdi and Hollingsworth conjectured in 1996 that every optimal colouring of $K_{2n}$ decomposes into rainbow spanning trees, and for many years only a constant number of disjoint ones could be found. Montgomery, Pokrovskiy and Sudakov [217] found $(1-o(1))n$ of them in every proper colouring, and Glock, Kühn, Montgomery and Osthus [137] completed the decomposition for optimal colourings (Theorem 3.7). Rota's basis conjecture of 1989 asks for the same kind of decomposition for $n$ bases of an $n$-dimensional vector space (Conjecture 3.16). Its matroid version contains the case of $n$ spanning trees (Figure 12). Montgomery and Sauermann [220] proved it asymptotically: $(1-o(1))n$ disjoint rainbow bases exist, and $(1+o(1))n$ of them cover everything (Theorem 3.18).

Paths have a shorter but similar history. A proper colouring of $K_n$ has at least $n-1$ colours, as many as a Hamilton path has edges, and in 1980 Hahn conjectured that a rainbow Hamilton path always exists (see [63]). In 1984 Maamoun and Meyniel [210] disproved this for every $n$ that is a power of $2$ (Figure 14). Andersen [36] conjectured in 1989 that a rainbow path missing only one vertex always exists. After a long sequence of approximate results, Bowtell, Montgomery, Müyesser and Pokrovskiy [63] proved this for large $n$ (Theorem 4.2). Orderings of group elements are rainbow paths in special colourings. In 1971 Graham [144] asked whether the elements of a subset of $\mathbb{Z}_p$ can be ordered so that all partial sums are distinct. Such an ordering is a rainbow directed path in the Cayley digraph of $\mathbb{Z}_p$, where the edge from $g$ to $g+s$ has colour $s$ (Conjecture 4.6, Figure 16).

Short rainbow cycles have a different origin and a different application. Behzad, Chartrand and Wall conjectured in 1970, and Caccetta and Häggkvist more generally in 1978, that digraphs with large outdegree have short directed cycles. Aharoni proposed a rainbow generalisation, and the Caccetta–Häggkvist conjecture follows from it by colouring each edge by its initial vertex (Section 4.3). In 2007 Keevash, Mubayi, Sudakov and Verstraëte [173] asked how many edges a properly coloured graph needs to contain a rainbow cycle. Bounds for this question have since been used in coding theory: Alrabiah and Guruswami [32] used rainbow cycles to prove near-tight lower bounds on the length of $3$-query locally correctable binary linear codes (Section 4.4).

Several translations reach other fields. In 1961 Erdős, Ginzburg and Ziv showed that any $2t-1$ integers contain $t$ with sum divisible by $t$ (see [22]). Aharoni and Berger [2] conjectured in 2009 that $2^{r-1}(t-1)+1$ matchings of size $t$ in an $r$-partite $r$-graph have a rainbow matching of size $t$. Alon [22] disproved this by turning zero-sum-free sequences into families of matchings (Figure 31), and Pohoata, Sauermann and Zakharov [235] showed that the right number grows like $t^r$ (Theorem 7.1). In fair division, rainbow matchings of hypergraphs decide when maximin share allocations exist (Figure 33, Theorem 7.5), and rainbow cycles in multipartite digraphs bound the number of goods left unallocated in approximate EFX allocations (Figure 26, Theorem 6.5). In design theory, the resolutions of Steiner triple systems go back to Kirkman's schoolgirl problem of 1850, and Keevash's theory of designs includes coloured decompositions such as rainbow triangle decompositions [172].

Two settings recur in every chapter. In a graph collection, one edge is taken from each of several graphs on the same vertex set; colouring each graph's edges with its own colour makes this a rainbow problem (Figure 13). Aharoni conjectured that $n$ graphs of minimum degree $n/2$ on the same $n$ vertices have a Hamilton cycle with one edge from each, and Joos and Kim [166] proved it. So the threshold is the same as for one graph, but Montgomery, Müyesser and Pehova [216] showed that for some factors the collection threshold is higher (Section 5.5). In random settings, Frieze and McKay [126] found rainbow spanning trees in randomly coloured random graphs as soon as enough colours and edges have appeared (Theorem 3.22). Han and Yuan [152] showed in general that a rainbow structure appears in a randomly coloured random set at most a logarithmic factor after the uncoloured one (Theorem 5.14). We treat both settings within the chapters on the corresponding structures.

Pokrovskiy [239] surveyed rainbow subgraphs and their applications, Montgomery [215] surveyed transversals in Latin squares, and Sudakov [254] surveyed restricted subgraphs of edge-coloured graphs with applications to coding theory. This survey is organised by the structure that is sought, and it includes developments since these surveys, many from 2025 and 2026. We do not cover anti-Ramsey numbers, rainbow Turán numbers or rainbow connection; the question of how many edges force a rainbow cycle is included because of its use in coding theory. Within each topic, constructions that rule out the natural statement come first, then the strongest positive results, and then a discussion of what remains open and what the next target would be.
